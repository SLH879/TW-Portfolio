# Participatory Research Methods and Critical Approaches to Community Engagement

Document type: Literature-based research essay | Originally written: December 2024 | Revised: October 2026 | Status: Draft

## Introduction
	Participatory research methods revolve around community involvement and the co-creation of knowledge. They disrupt traditional research hierarchies that clearly distinguish researchers from participants/respondents by fostering collaborative partnerships among researchers and communities, allowing for research to be culturally sensitive, inclusive, and beneficial to studied communities while minimizing harm (Reid et al., 2017). These partnerships over rigid institutional frameworks allow for collaborative partnerships, reciprocity, and ethics revolving around relationships (Banks et al., 2013). They emphasize democratic participation, ethical responsibility, nuanced understanding of unique lived realities, and the recognition of the value of local and unique knowledge systems (von Lieres, 2024; Banks et al., 2013). The continued reflection on the communities and upholding research accountability and ethicality make it a fundamentally important tool in empowering communities whose voices would otherwise be ignored and overlooked, challenging power dynamics equitably, and fostering knowledge creation through collaboration and social construction (von Lieres, 2024). 

	Therefore, participatory research methods serve as critical transformative tools in critical approaches to community engagement by empowering marginalized voices, challenging power dynamics, and fostering collaborative knowledge creation that recognizes and highlights local contexts. 

## Empowerment of Marginalized Voices
	First, participatory research is important in empowering marginalized voices because it aligns with ethical principles of respecting for persons, concerning welfare, and upholding social justice (Reid et al., 2017). In contrast to traditional research methods, participatory research is rooted in power sharing, collaborations, and upholding community rights through blurring the boundaries of researcher and participants, allowing for a more democratic approach to the cocreation of knowledge and learning. Underrepresented groups such as Indigenous communities, racial and gender minorities, and many others are typically in positions of marginalization, where they are overlooked, ignored, and stripped of powers and agency (Minkler & Wallerstein, 2008; Reid et al., 2017). Through participatory research, these groups are given the opportunities to share their voices, ensuring their needs and perspectives are central to engagement, and allowing for local contexts to be considered when framing research questions and potential solutions. Specifically, this approach aims to ensure that knowledge is holistic, incorporating lived experiences of the local communities, and ensuring that the research is community driven and in deep collaboration with the communities in an effort to respect traditional knowledge and adhere to community protocols (Reid et al., 2017). By ensuring that community members are able to actively shape the research processes, this allows for research outcomes that are mutually beneficial to marginalized communities as well as ensure they are culturally relevant.  

	Furthermore, beyond empowerment and giving marginalized communities opportunities to share their narratives, participatory research also aims to protect the well-being of these communities. Guided by the Core Principles of Research Ethics (TCPS), participatory research as an approach aims to recognize the participants’ autonomy and ensure that they are participating freely while being informed and consenting to data collection (Reid et al., 2017). It ensures that their physical, emotional, and social well-being are protected, that they are treated fairly and equitably in relation to everyone else, and that power balances are addressed proactively and in a just manner (Reid et al., 2017).  

	Though, it is also important for participatory researchers to recognize that existing internal and external biases should be controlled. Internal biases such as assumptions and relationships with different communities, and external biases such as dominant social narratives (master narratives) about different races and genders can all influence attitudes, behaviours, and interpretations (McCorkel & Myers, 2003). Especially when in a position of power as the researcher, these existing biases can threaten accuracy of research and interpretations, and may potentially be at risk of creating more harm than benefit to the research and the community participants (McCorkel & Myers, 2003). The threat of such biases calls for the need to reflect on how a researcher’s standpoint can shape the research process, and the importance of self reflection to better understand one’s existing biases (Banks et al., 2013).

## Challenging Existing Power Dynamics
	Next, participatory research is critical to community engagement because it challenges existing power dynamics. In traditional hierarchies, researchers tend to dominate decision making as detached experts and authority figures with more power, reinforcing a clear distinction between researchers and community partners (McCorkel & Myers, 2003). This allows them to have much more leverage over how researches proceed. However, the position of a researcher, especially those in privileged social positions, can potentially introduce biases in assumptions and how they engage with different community respondents, affecting the validity and accuracy of data collection, analysis, and interpretation (McCorkel & Myers, 2003). This can lead to the marginalization of community voices, overlooking unique perspectives over those of the collective, and ultimately placing master narratives and research findings above local and individual wisdom (Banks et al., 2013). Especially with how complex hierarchies of privilege and disadvantage can be in creating unique, individual, and context specific nuances from dimensions such as race and gender, it is important for researchers to recognize the intersectional nature of these systems within the participatory research framework (Minkler & Wallerstein, 2008). 

	Recognizing this imbalance, participatory research aims to push for more relevant and context specific narratives, challenging existing societal dominant ones. This is achieved by valuing Indigenous and local knowledge systems as valid and essential to driving meaningful research outcomes. Instead of maintaining a clear distinction between researcher and partners, researcher roles are re-defined with participatory research, allowing dual roles to be allotted to them: both advocate and researcher (Banks et al., 2013), allowing for collaborative decision making and shared authority with the community partners. Specifically, it allows for active listening of hidden and public narratives within communities, further adding to the nuanced understandings of different racial and ethnic groups (Minkler & Wallerstein, 2008). As an advocate interacting with communities daily, it stresses for the need to regularly engage in self reflections and acknowledgements of personal prejudices, which can also contribute to the nuanced understandings of these groups (McCorkel & Myers, 2003). In the process, this allows for mutual learning, allowing researchers to recognize and work towards addressing community needs while also upholding research standards. 

	Overall, this dual role that participatory research introduces allow researchers to be able to address power imbalances and injustices, helping empower marginalized communities through making sure their voices are heard with the goals of educating and shifting existing societal dominant narratives, and ultimately predict more equitable outcomes for these communities.  

## Critiquing Regulatory Ethics Frameworks
	Furthermore, it is also important to note that participatory research also challenges power dynamics by critiquing existing regulatory ethics framework. These existing frameworks such as the institutional review boards (IRBs), tend to be rigid and rule based. The reliance on standardized protocols for traditional research models often overlook the shared decision making and flexible processes that participatory research often revolves around. With inflexible procedures in place that do not recognize the ever-evolving processes that is participatory research, IRBs limit the abilities to effectively address power imbalances where researchers and institutions have much more power, authority, and decision-making powers than communities (Banks et al., 2013). These frameworks are insufficient for the community-based nature of participatory research, which revolves around continued negotiating, adapting, and sharing of power (Minkler & Wallerstein, 2008). Due to its rigidity, cultural and social nuances such as racism and oppression from the three-tiered framework of racism, including institutionalized racism like employment, healthcare and housing, personally mediated racism like stereotyping based on race, and internalized racism like self blame which prevents recognition of merits and potentials, may be overlooked (Minkler & Wallerstein, 2008). Without giving in-depth considerations to nuanced power dynamics perpetuated by these institutionalized racism and inequities, lived realities of marginalized communities continue to be overlooked and not heard (Banks et al., 2013).

	Participatory research aims to challenge this by identifying involved communities as equal partners in the research process, recognizing their lived experiences as important to ethical research. This approach helps dismantle oppressive structures by recognizing that rigid blanket approaches cannot be adopted, and that acknowledging nuances is very important in better understanding different communities (McCorkel & Myers, 2003). This allows the recognition of the tiered racism and oppression that marginalized communities may be subject to. It upholds shared decision-making authorities and co-ownership of knowledge to ensure participants retain agency and autonomy throughout the research process. Moreover, it urges the broader research community to rethink how relationships, responsibilities, and contexts are approached through the lens of everyday ethics (Banks et al., 2013). As highlighted by Banks et al. (2013), everyday ethics focuses on ethical decision-making processes that uphold relationship, trust and respect over strict adherence to formal and rigid rules. This perspective recognizes that participatory research is context specific and requires adaptability, ongoing dialogue, and awareness of power imbalances. Overall, participatory research fosters an equal and collaborative environment that values diverse knowledge systems and promote a more flexible approach to research, allowing ethical practices and community agency to be upheld throughout the research processes.

## Collaborative Knowledge Creation
	Finally, participatory research allows for collaborative knowledge creation that recognizes local contexts. As a transformative tool, it allows for the co-creation of knowledge between researchers and community members, allowing for mutual learning and the acknowledgement of lived experiences, critically understanding how local contexts, and internal and external factors can shape lived realities (McCorkel & Myers, 2003; Minkler & Wallerstein, 2008). 

	The consideration of local contexts ensure research findings are authentic and accurate to different contexts, recognizing that communities are diverse and shaped by many external factors such as the interlocking systems of racism and oppression (Minkler & Wallerstein, 2008). These local contexts are able to reflect the different social, cultural, and historical conditions that specific communities have been subject to that may not be shared elsewhere (von Lieres, 2024). With the varying degrees of exposure to both internal and external factors, communities are vastly different. Moving beyond master narratives that have been established by society, working in closely knit communities allow for more personalized insights to be heard. By centering the voices of different communities, participatory research allows for both silenced and public voices to be heard, allowing for a more nuanced and comprehensive narratives to be developed.

	When it comes to collaborative knowledge creation, blanket approaches cannot be adopted as they are insufficient in understanding these communities. An extension to the above critiqued IRBs, such approaches can overlook relevant realities of specific communities. The differences in access to power, opportunities, and exposure to varying levels of systemic oppression all necessitate the importance of context specific research approaches that better reflect the nuanced realities of different communities. Since truth is created and knowledge socially constructed (von Lieres, 2024), participatory research should therefore seek to remedy this barrier by involving community partners as equal partners in knowledge creation, ensuring that relevant and lived realities are reflected in research, while also upholding the core principles of respect, concern, and justice for communities (Reid et al., 2017). 

## Conclusion
	In conclusion, participatory research methods play pivotal roles in critical approaches to community engagement. Participatory research empowers marginalized community voices, recognizing the importance of both hidden and public voices. This allows for suppressed voices and local contexts to be considered, promoting culturally sensitive and inclusive outcomes. Furthermore, challenging existing power dynamics by disrupting hierarchies of researcher and participants, and allowing unique community narratives to be heard beyond already established social master narratives. This allows for unique perspectives and insights to be developed, shifting existing understandings of different groups of people. In addition, this paper has also critiqued existing regulatory ethics frameworks, demonstrating that due to the ever-changing nature of communities, flexible and personalized frameworks that prioritize relationships and community agency should be employed instead. Finally, participatory research serves as a transformative tool for the co-creation of knowledge, allowing for nuanced realities of diverse communities to be acknowledged, ensuring research data can be grounded, relevant, and context-specific. 
	
	Overall, this paper has highlighted the impactful roles that participatory research can play in community engagement, and how it is critical in also bringing about positive change to empowerment, power imbalances, rigid rules that overlook nuanced struggles and dynamics, and the co-creation of socially constructed and mutually learnt knowledge.
